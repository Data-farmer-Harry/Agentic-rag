Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) and agentic AI systems have evolved from natural language tasks to using external
tools to plan, retrieve, and act in technical domains. In smart grids, recent work applies agentic schemes to forecasting,
optimization, and control, wrapping trusted solvers behind language interfaces and orchestrating multi-step workflows.
The literature lacks a unified approach to designing and evaluating such systems. LLMs can produce numerically
plausible yet physically infeasible outputs, evaluation protocols vary across tasks, and the boundary between what
the model should and should not compute is implicit. This paper presents asolver-grounded designprinciple: a
numerical result is reported only when it originates from a trusted tool and passes explicit verification. We review the
building blocks of LLM and agentic AI systems for power systems: prompting strategies and agentic architectures. We
instantiate the principle in four case studies: wind power forecasting, EV charging scheduling, power flow analysis, and
contingency diagnosis, each comparing an LLM-only baseline against its solver-grounded counterpart on identical data
and metrics. EV Agent reproduces the CVXPY optimum while reducing LLM-only unmet energy by 7.5–9.5×, and
GridDebugAgent repairs 17/39 contingency cases while reducing total violations by 52.3%. We propose a four-group
evaluation framework spanning task utility, solver-grounded correctness, faithfulness and safe failure, and cost and
latency. A consistent division of labor emerges, the agentic system reliably orchestrates, retrieves, and explains, while
trusted tools compute and a verification gate decides what is reported.
Keywords:Agentic AI, Large language models, Smart grids, Power systems
1. Introduction
Adoption of large language models (LLMs) and agentic AI systems has accelerated across research and industry.
Google alone reported processing over 3.2 quadrillion tokens per month across its products in mid-2026, a sevenfold
year-over-year increase [1], and Gartner projects that 40% of enterprise applications will embed task-specific AI agents
by the end of 2026, up from less than 5% a year earlier, with agentic AI accounting for an estimated $450 billion in
enterprise-software revenue by 2035 [2]. These systems are now widely adopted for software engineering [3], scientific
discovery [4], robotics [5], and domain-specific decision support [6]. Agentic systems combine LLMs with external
tools, memory, retrieval, code execution, and trusted solvers so the LLM acts as planner and orchestrator rather than
the sole source of answers [ 7, 8, 9, 10]. This shift from a single model call to a tool-use loop lets LLMs engage
with multi-step engineering workflows where text alone is not enough. Agentic systems can decompose a problem,
orchestrate solvers and data sources, inspect results, and revise plans, but these skills have barely reached engineering
workflows that demand verified, physically grounded computation.
Many power and energy systems tasks align closely with such an agentic workflow. Grid planning and operation,
for example, combines language, data, physical models, optimization, and control in one procedure, where an operator

no basis in underlying systems [20], and have no built-in mechanisms to enforce physical laws such as power flow

violating these constraints without flagging it [ 11, 12]. LLM outputs are also hard to verify by inspection, since a
correct numerical claim and an incorrect one are written with the same confidence and format, so an operator cannot
judge credibility without re-running the trusted tool. This matters because grid outputs feed safety-critical control
actions: a misreported voltage limit propagates into a dispatch decision, an under-delivered EV schedule becomes
customer complaints and bill charges, and a missed contingency violation can damage equipment or cause blackouts.
Power systems therefore need a deployment principle distinct from open-ended AI chatbots, one in which numerical
claims are produced by trusted tools and gated by explicit verification before they reach the operator.
This paper takes this principle as its central thesis: LLMs and agentic AI systems for smart grids should be designed
as a solver-grounded orchestration layer. The proposed design separates three roles: TheLLMinterprets the request,
plans the task, calls tools, and generates the response. Thetrusted toolperforms the numerical computation. The
verification gatechecks the result against physical and operational constraints before any value reaches the operator.
Case studies in this paper span distinct smart grid task types, from forecasting and optimization to network analysis and
diagnosis, and each is a setting where a plain LLM tends to produce plausible but infeasible numbers, so the solver-
grounded design offers the clearest advantage. Fig. 1 illustrates this structure alongside the conventional power-system

1. We present a layered tutorial on LLM-based and agentic systems for smart grids, spanning prompting strategies
(structured, few-shot, chain-of-thought, and RAG) and agentic architectures together with their engineering
ingredients (typed tool interfaces, short- and long-term memory, and orchestration frameworks).
2. We formalize the solver-grounded design rule: a numerical result is reported only when it originates from a

against its solver-grounded counterpart on identical data and metrics, with reproducible results in Section 6.
3. We propose a four-group evaluation framework covering task utility, solver-grounded correctness, faithfulness
and safe failure, and cost and latency. All four groups are defined in Section 2.3. The framework supports
head-to-head comparison across architectures and surfaces failure modes that task utility alone would hide.

The next-token-prediction objective in (1)–(2) has two implications for LLM-based smart grid systems. First, since

physical, operational, or numerical correctness. LLMs are trained to model patterns in large text corpora, not to
guarantee physical laws such as Kirchhoff’s laws or optimization feasibility in power grids. The model also has
no intrinsic way to distinguish quantities computed by a trusted solver from quantities inferred through language
generation, and both appear equally fluent in the output. This explains why an LLM can produce a plausible but
infeasible dispatch or an incorrect power flow result, and motivates the solver-grounded design rule of Section 2.3,
where correctness is enforced with trusted tools and verification.
2.2. Frontier LLM Families and Training Procedures
Frontier LLMs split intoproprietaryfamilies accessed through managed APIs andopen-weightfamilies deployable
locally, the latter suitable when operational data is not allowed to be accessed out of the utility’s network [50, 51, 52,
53, 54, 55, 56]. Across both, context length, multimodal-input support, and structured-output reliability vary by family
and model size, and the model landscape evolves on a fast timescale of months.
The frontier models share a common three-stage training pipeline: self-supervised pretraining on raw text, super-
vised fine-tuning on instruction-response data, and preference-based alignment.
In thepretraining stage, the model learns broad statistical patterns of language by minimizing the next-token
loss in (2) over a large unlabeled corpus. This gives general linguistic competence and world knowledge but targets
no specific task or instruction format, so a pretrained-only model continues text fluently yet may not reliably follow
instructions, answer in the expected format, or refuse unsafe requests.
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)then adapts the pretrained model to instruction following using curated prompt-
response demonstrations [57], updating the parameters by minimizing the token-level cross-entropy between predicted
and target responses. SFT lets LLMs give structured, formatted answers and is a prerequisite for the tool calling,
structured outputs, and conversational behavior agentic systems rely on.
A final alignment stage optimizes the SFT model with human preference feedback so responses better match
expectations for helpfulness, safety, and reliability [58]. The most common implementation isreinforcement learning
from human feedback (RLHF), where a reward model trained on human preference comparisons is used to optimize the
LLM through reinforcement learning. A related approach,reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR),

2.3. The Solver-grounded Design Rule
The central design rule of this paper is that LLMs and agents should not be treated as sources of numerical truth in
smart grid applications. Instead they act as interfaces, task planners, and orchestrators around trusted computational
tools. These tools are central to safety-critical power systems practice, such as operating and planning studies already
rely on power-flow solvers, OPF solvers, economic-dispatch optimizers, forecasting models, and simulators to enforce
physical laws, operational limits, and feasibility. In current workflows, engineers configure cases, run these tools,
inspect convergence and constraint violations, iterate across scenarios, and translate the outputs into operational reports.
LLMs fit tool calling naturally because they can map natural language requests into structured tool inputs, select
and sequence the right solver or data API, interpret logs and outputs, and explain results while leaving numerical
computation to verified softwares. A numerical result should therefore be reported only if a trusted solver or simulator
produces it and it passes explicit verification. This separates three roles. The LLM interprets the request, decomposes
the task, selects tools, and explains results. The trusted tool performs the numerical computation. The verification gate
checks whether the output is physically and operationally valid. Formally, let{Tk}K

One boundary deserves explicit statement. Atrusted toolhere does not mean an infallible source of truth. Instead,
such a tool refers to a model, solver, or simulator whose assumptions are explicit and whose outputs can be checked for
convergence, feasibility, and consistency with power-system constraints. This matches current practice: engineers rely

constraint residuals, voltage and line-flow limits, and input assumptions before using the results operationally.
Such a principle applies most directly when such a tool exists for the reported quantity, as in the EV scheduling,
power flow, and contingency case studies, where a solver computes the result and the verification gate checks its validity
for the specified model and data. When no trusted solver exists, as in LLM-based wind power forecasting, the output
cannot be solver-grounded in the same sense, and verification is limited to model- and output-level checks such as
schema validity, range feasibility, consistency with historical data, and forecast-error evaluation against observations.
This is the boundary of the solver-grounded regime rather than an exception: absent a trusted tool, the system should
report predictions with their validation evidence and uncertainty, not as solver-verified quantities.
2.4. Where LLMs and Agents Add the Most Value
The solver-grounded rule of Section 2.3 guardrails what LLMs should not do in smart grid tasks. In practice,
there are several recurring bottlenecks in solver-grounded smart grid workflows: data preparation, model specification,
tool-run configuration, failure detection, scenario iteration, and technical reporting and interpretation. We do not
propose these as a formal taxonomy but as a high-level synthesis of workflow patterns that motivate the observations
and case studies in Section 6.
First, bottlenecks live around rather than inside the solver.Data preparation, model setup, iteration, and reporting
consume most expert time per scenario, while the solver call is fast. This supports positioning the LLM as an
interface and orchestration layer rather than a numerical engine. The PFAgent case study (Section 6.3) shows the split:
PandaPower’s Newton-Raphson solver computes the operating point, while the LLM parses multi-step queries, selects
and sequences tools, tracks network state across turns, and synthesizes outputs into operator-readable responses.
Second, iteration cost compounds with case complexity.As grid studies grow from one case to many scenarios,
contingencies, assets, and operating conditions, the burden shifts from solving one well-posed problem to repeatedly
specifying tasks, selecting tools, checking inputs, interpreting outputs, and revising the workflow. Here LLMs and
agents add value as planners and orchestrators, since they decompose a request into subtasks, identify the required
data and tools, sequence tool calls, and adapt to intermediate results, leaving numerical computation to trusted solvers.
The GridDebugAgent case study (Section 6.4) shows this by automating the diagnose-act-verify loop while preserving
solver-grounded correctness.
Third, verification itself is a bottleneck.The final output often combines numerical results, explanations, diagnostics,
and recommendations. Even when the solver call is correct, the agent can misreport a value, omit a violation, or
overstate a result’s validity. A solver-grounded design therefore separates three functions: i). the LLM interprets, plans,
orchestrates, and explains, ii). trusted tools produce the numerical evidence, and iii). a verification gate decides whether
the final answer is safe to communicate. Case studies quantify these roles, where EV Agent produces zero hard physical
constraint violation across 20 benchmark days, whereas a standalone LLM baseline violates the 50 kW site cap.

3.3. Chain-of-thought Prompting
Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting asks the model to produce intermediate reasoning steps before the final
answer [64]. Because generation is sequential, the steps it writes become context for the final answer, so it decomposes
a multi-step problem into sub-steps and builds the answer on its own reasoning. Making the steps explicit lets a reader
follow how the answer was reached and improves quality on multi-step tasks. Fig. 4 illustrates CoT prompting on a
power flow over-voltage diagnosis.
In smart grid applications, CoT helps when the LLM explains solver outputs or supports human review. For a
bus over-voltage, a CoT response can identify the violated limit, locate the affected bus, connect it to causes such as
excessive local generation, and suggest actions such as adjusting inverter setpoints or transformer taps. Like the other
techniques, CoT does not guarantee correct reasoning. It can produce plausible reasoning while making an arithmetic
error or recommending an infeasible action, so the final response must still be checked against trusted computations
and constraints. In power systems, CoT has been used within LLM assistants for dispatch operations [33].

3.4. Retrieval-augmented Generation (RAG)
RAG conditions the output on external documents retrieved at inference time [65]. An LLM answering from its
internalized memory alone can produce plausible but outdated or unsupported statements. RAG addresses this in two
stages. A retriever first searches an external knowledge source for the passages most relevant to the query. Those
passages are inserted into the prompt as context, so the model answers from the retrieved evidence rather than its
parameters alone, often citing the passages used. In practice, source documents are split into self-contained chunks,
retrieved with metadata filters such as jurisdiction, voltage level, or effective date, and reranked for relevance. The
prompt must return an explicit “insufficient evidence” response when the passages do not support an answer. Fig. 5
illustrates the pipeline on a grid-code compliance question.
In smart grid applications, RAG helps because many task-relevant facts are not reliably stored in model parameters,
including operating procedures, grid codes, market rules, reliability standards, tariffdocuments, and equipment manuals.

manual, or utility procedure. A RAG system first retrieves the relevant document sections, such as the applicable
voltage range, effective date, or operating condition. The prompt then asks the model to summarize the rule, decide
whether the action satisfies it, and cite the supporting passage. This matters most when the answer depends on local
rules or current documentation rather than general power-system knowledge. In power systems, RAG has grounded
operational-reliability assessment in reliability documentation and operating procedures [39].

4. Agentic Systems for Smart Grids
Moving from prompting to agentic systems, the LLM is no longer restricted to its input context but orchestrates
external tools, inspects intermediate results, and decides what to do next. We first define an agentic system and
describe how it acts on the environment through API calls and tool use. We then cover five representative architectures:
Reasoning and Acting (ReAct), plan-and-act, hierarchical, self-reflection, and multi-agent systems. We close with two
cross-cutting components every architecture relies on: memory, which preserves state across steps, and the frameworks
used to build agents in practice.
4.1. Agentic System Definition
An agentic system extends the LLM beyond a single forward pass: instead of producing the answer directly from
its input context, it solves a task through a sequence of reasoning steps and actions, where each action can query an
external tool and return a new observation [7]. A common representation is the ReAct trajectory
τt =(x,Thought 1,Action 1,Observation 1,...,Thought t,Action t,Observation t),(6)
where x is the initial user input or task description andτt is the partial trajectory up to step t. At each step, Actiont∈A
is drawn from an action space A that includes LLM reasoning steps, such as decomposing the task or drafting a
candidate answer, and external tool invocations, such as calling a power flow solver, querying a database, or executing a
script. The resulting Observationt∈O may come from an external tool, the operating environment, or the LLM’s own
reasoning traces when intermediate thoughts and self-evaluations are fed back into later steps. The agent interleaves
reasoning, action selection, tool interaction, and observation updates, and at each step decides whether to continue or
terminate with a final answer or recommended action.
This loop matches many smart grid tasks, where the system cannot answer from internal knowledge alone and must
call a solver, retrieve external information, and check intermediate results before reporting. Consider N−1 contingency
screening, where the agent is asked whether the system can survive the loss of a specified transmission line. It first
retrieves the current network topology and operating point, then invokes a power flow solver under the post-contingency
configuration, inspects the returned bus voltages and line flows, and determines whether any operating limit is violated.
If needed, it calls an OPF routine to evaluate corrective actions. Each step depends on the previous observation, and the
number of steps is not known in advance.

4.2. Action: API Calls and Tool Use
Tool use is central to solver-grounded smart grid workflows [7, 8]. The principle is that whenever a task requires
real-time data, exact computation, or external documents, the agent should call a tool rather than infer the answer
from text patterns. In power systems, such tools include data APIs, power flow simulators, optimization solvers,
and verification routines. The LLM selects the tool, formulates its input, interprets the output, and reports the result
faithfully, while the tool provides the evidence. A practical tool interface makes this contract explicit through input and
output schemas, unit conventions, and failure messages, which reduce common errors such as loading the wrong case
or reporting a wrong unit.
It helps to distinguishread toolsfromwrite tools. Read tools retrieve or compute information without changing the
environment, such as fetching weather forecasts, market prices, or event logs. Write tools modify it, for example by
sending a control command, updating a schedule, or submitting a market bid. Because write tools create operational
side effects, they should sit behind safeguards such as simulation, rule checking, approval workflows, or human-in-
the-loop confirmation, which let agents support analysis, planning, and decision preparation while limiting unsafe or
unauthorized actions. Tool use in smart grid agents is thus best understood as a task-specific grounding mechanism, not
a generic capability: forecasting agents rely on data APIs and time-series models, scheduling agents on optimization
solvers and feasibility checkers, and grid-analysis agents on power flow simulators, diagnostic parsers, and visualization
modules. Table 2 summarizes representative tool families and their roles.

separation suits workflows with clear stage boundaries, where each stage has a different cost profile. In smart grids,
planning studies such as data center siting or transmission expansion planning fit naturally. The agent plans the full
sequence of stages, then executes each in turn, replanning only if a stage fails. Its limitation is that the run is only as
good as the upfront plan. When the setting is hard to anticipate, an inaccurate plan forces repeated replanning.

coordinate and merge worker outputs, and a poor decomposition degrades the answer.

4.4. Memory Management
Memory lets an agent preserve state across steps: what has been tried, which tools were called, what observations
returned, and whether outputs passed verification. It should be treated as a structured system component, not just a
longer conversation history [9, 69, 70]. It includesshort-term memory, holding state within a single trajectory, and
long-term memory, which carries across trajectories and holds episodic and semantic memories.
Short-term memoryholds the working state within a single task: a scratchpad recording the current goal, steps
taken, tool outputs received, and intermediate conclusions. Each reasoning step reads from it and writes back new
information. Because it persists only for one trajectory, it is most exposed to context-window pressure: when the
working state grows too large, items can be dropped or paraphrased, silently corrupting the reasoning [70]. In smart
grid workflows, it keeps operational details such as the active network case and the latest solver output accessible
across steps within a task.

4.5. Frameworks and Tools for Building Agents
Frameworks for agentic AI provide the engineering layer for defining agents, registering tools, managing memory,
and deploying workflows. We group them by what they orchestrate, since the unit of orchestration most shapes how a
smart grid workflow is built. Three classes differ in this unit: a single agent’s loop, a team of agents, or an explicit
workflow graph, all relying on a connectivity layer of interoperability protocols. Because the ecosystem changes
quickly, we describe each class by its durable engineering role and name representative tools only as illustrations.
Agent SDKs and middleware orchestrate a single agent’s loop, with lightweight abstractions for tool use and
logging (OpenAI Agents SDK [71], Semantic Kernel [72], Strands Agents [73]). They are the fastest to adopt but leave
control flow implicit. Multi-agent frameworks orchestrate a team of agents, with abstractions for inter-agent messaging
and role assignment (AutoGen [10], CrewAI [74]). They suit problems that decompose into distinct roles. Workflow
orchestrators run an explicit graph of steps, with declared transitions, retries, and checkpoints (LangGraph [ 75],
Microsoft Agent Framework [76]). They give the most control at the cost of more setup, and their explicit steps make
a natural place to insert a verification gate. The three classes are not mutually exclusive. Interoperability protocols
form the connectivity layer, standardizing agent-to-tool and agent-to-agent connections (MCP [77], A2A [78]). They
orchestrate and verify nothing themselves but provide the substrate the framework classes use when external tools or
remote agents enter the loop.
In practice, power-system agents have favored lightweight custom orchestration over heavyweight frameworks.
Most are bespoke single-agent loops with direct function calling, as in GridDebugAgent and PFAgent, or multi-
agent designs that separate planning from validation, as in GridMind [13] and Grid-Agent [14]. At the connectivity
layer, MCP is emerging as the common substrate, adopted by X-GridAgent [27] and advocated in the PowerAgent
roadmap [16]. Two patterns stand out. Framework choice is rarely standardized or reported across studies, which
complicates reproducibility. Verification is seldom part of the framework layer, so it must be added explicitly.
The unit of orchestration guides which framework class to choose:
• Agent SDKsfor single-agent assistants, such as operator copilots, report generation, and the conversational
PFAgent and GridDebugAgent of our case studies.
• Multi-agent frameworkswhen the workflow decomposes into distinct roles, as in GridMind and Grid-Agent,
which separate planning from validation.
• Workflow orchestratorswhen a task has clear stage boundaries and steps must be verified before execution,
such as multi-stage planning studies orN−1 contingency screening.
• Interoperability protocolswhenever external tools or remote agents must connect, as in the MCP-based interface
of X-GridAgent, independent of which framework orchestrates the task.
In every case the framework supplies only the engineering layer: solver-grounded correctness still rests on the
verification gate and trusted tools of Section 2, not the framework, and Section 5 discusses evaluation and verification.
5. Evaluation Protocol
Evaluation of LLM-based and agentic systems for smart grids must match the solver-grounded workflow of
Section 2. A system that produces fluent but unverified numbers can score well on standard language metrics while
still issuing physically infeasible decisions, and existing evaluation efforts were not designed for this setting. General
agent benchmarks measure end-task success and tool-use competence but stay domain-agnostic and do not tie a
tool’s output to physical feasibility [ 79]. RAG metrics assess faithfulness to retrieved text rather than to a trusted
solver [80]. Selective-prediction criteria formalize abstention for classification rather than for verification-gated
numerical reporting [ 60]. Recent surveys confirm that agent evaluation remains fragmented, and that agent-level
hallucinations propagate across tool calls and reasoning steps in ways single-turn metrics miss [ 81, 82]. To our

knowledge, no existing framework couples these concerns into one protocol linking tool-output correctness to grid
feasibility, the gap the protocol below addresses.
We organize the evaluation around four metric groups that follow from the solver-grounded rule (Section 2.3). The
rule reports a number only when a trusted tool computes it and verification passes. A numerical claim can therefore fail
at three successive points on its path from computation to report. It can be inaccurate on the objective, infeasible at the
tool boundary, or misreported in the final text. Producing a verified answer also consumes resources such as tokens,
solver compute, and time. These four concerns define the groups. (i)Task utilityis accuracy on the primary modeling
or optimization objective. (ii)Solver-grounded correctnessis whether the solution respects physical and operational
constraints at the tool boundary. (iii)Faithfulness and safe failureis whether the natural-language report transmits the
verified output without distortion and abstains when verification fails. (iv)Cost and latencyis the resources required to
produce a verified answer.
Each group exposes a failure mode invisible to the others. A low-error forecast can still violate a constraint, a
feasible solver result can still be misreported, and a correct, faithful answer can still be too slow or costly to deploy.
Groups (i) to (iii) exhaust the points at which a numerical claim can go wrong under the rule, while (iv) is the one
concern orthogonal to correctness that gates deployment. Task utility alone, the metric most often reported in prior
capability studies [11, 12], hides exactly the constraint violations and report-level hallucinations that groups (ii) and
(iii) expose.
5.1. Task utility
Task utility measures how good an answer is on the problem’s own terms, independent of how it was produced. It is
the score we would assign if no LLM or agent were involved, reported identically for the LLM-only and solver-grounded
variants so the two are directly comparable. For regression and forecasting tasks, we report a prediction-error metric
against ground truth, such as mean absolute error (MAE), root-mean-square error (RMSE), or mean absolute percentage
error. For classification and detection subtasks, we report an accuracy- or overlap-based metric such as F1 score. For
constrained optimization, we report the achieved objective as an optimality gap relative to the optimal solver solution,
paired with a task-completion metric such as the fraction of demand served. For diagnosis and remediation, we report
a success metric such as repair rate with a partial-progress metric such as violation reduction. These families cover
the most common smart grid tasks, and a task outside them adopts the native metric for its objective. Task utility is
necessary but not sufficient. A standalone LLM can minimize MAE by interpolating recent history while still violating
physical constraints, which is why the remaining three groups are needed. Some tasks are multi-objective, so a single
scalar does not suffice. EV scheduling trades cost against unmet energy, and power flow reports voltage, flow, and
balance errors. For these, task utility is a small vector of complementary metrics rather than one number.

6.4. GridDebugAgent: LLM-Assisted Contingency Diagnosis
Problem Description.Power grid operators face failures such as non-convergent load flow, voltage violations (outside
0.95–1.05 p.u.), and thermal overloads (>100% line loading). Diagnosing root causes and finding corrective actions
requires reasoning over network topology, running what-if simulations, and verifying repairs, tasks well suited to LLM
agents. We implementGridDebugAgent, a solver-grounded ReAct agent [ 7] that iteratively diagnoses failures and
executes remediation actions on electrical networks using the PandaPower library [87].
Strategy.GridDebugAgent has three components: (1) apreprocessing pipelinewith an evidence collector and rule
engine that extracts network metrics and classifies failures, (2) aReAct agent coreusing GPT-4o (temp. 0.3, max 50
iterations) with OpenAI function calling, and (3) a tool suite covering five categories: query, simulation, diagnostic,
grid actions, and memory management. GPT-4o is the backbone because the agent is built on OpenAI function calling,
for which GPT-4o offered strong tool-use support when the system was built. The design is model-agnostic, and the
PFAgent and EV Agent case studies exercise the same pattern across other frontier models. The grid-action tools include
generation adjustment, load curtailment, line/shunt switching, and voltage control, while memory tools support network
snapshots and conversation logging. Given a scenario, GridDebugAgent first applies diagnostic rules, then runs a
diagnose→act→verify loop: it queries network state, proposes corrective actions (e.g., adjusting generator setpoints),
executes them, re-runs power flow, and checks for remaining violations until the network is secure or max iterations are
reached. Figure 11 shows a successful 2-round repair on a line-outage contingency.

7. Conclusion and Discussions
This tutorial paper presents an implementation-grounded tutorial on LLM-based and agentic systems for smart
grids. Written for a power-systems audience, it reviews fundamental building blocks: prompting strategies and agentic
architectures, with their memory, tool interfaces, and orchestration frameworks. On this foundation we propose the
solver-grounded design rule, stating that a numerical result is reported only when it originates from a trusted tool
and passes explicit verification. A four-group evaluation protocol spanning task utility, solver-grounded correctness,
faithfulness and safe failure, and cost and latency is introduced. We instantiate both the rule and the protocol in four
end-to-end case studies: wind power forecasting, EV charging scheduling, power flow analysis, and contingency
diagnosis, each comparing an LLM-only baseline against its solver-grounded counterpart on identical data and metrics.
A consistent pattern emerged. When the LLM is the interface and a trusted tool is the source of truth, the agent inherits
the tool’s correctness. When the LLM is asked to compute, it does not. When no solver exists, as in forecasting,
verification degrades from physical feasibility to output-level schema and range checks.
Three limitations frame these findings. First, the case studies use off-the-shelf frontier LLMs without domain
adaptation, so the comparisons measure prompting and architecture rather than fine-tuning. Second, they cover four
representative tasks, not the full design space. Real-time control, market settlement, and protection coordination remain
open. Third, the protocol audits each system against its own solver-grounded counterpart rather than comparing two
agents head-to-head. The most consequential future work follows from these gaps. Reusable verification libraries
could expose per-unit, power-balance, and convergence checks behind a typed interface. Repair policies could turn the
safe-failure path into a recoverable one without weakening verification. Verifiable-reward fine-tuning [59] could reuse
verification-passed trajectories to train smaller tool-calling agents that run inside utility networks, keeping operational
data on premises. None of these replace solvers. They reorganize the work around them by parsing the request,
retrieving context, sequencing tool calls, and explaining a verified result. The operational impact of these systems,
in our reading, will come not from new numerical capability but from compressing the engineering hours between a
question an operator asks in natural language and an answer a control system can act on.

2. Failure Analysis Across Case Studies
Table S2 documents the scenarios where each system reaches its limit. EV Agent and PFAgent preserve the solver
result on every evaluated case, so their hard failures are confined to the LLM-only baselines. GridDebugAgent is
where the agentic framework itself leaves scenarios unsolved, and wind forecasting has no solver, so its failures are
prompting-level.
Every failure above is measured, not narrated. The shared checker recomputes each metric on the system the agent
produced, so partial repairs and residual violations surface in the reported numbers rather than being masked. This is
the safe-failure behavior of Section 2.3 in practice: the system reports the shortfall instead of fabricating a fix.

6. Design Checklist for Solver-Grounded Smart-Grid Agents
We distill the tutorial into a checklist a practitioner can apply when building an LLM or agent for a smart-grid task.
1. Separate the roles.Let the LLM parse, retrieve, plan, orchestrate, and explain, never compute physical quantities. Assign every
numerical result to a trusted solver or simulator (Section 2.3).
2. Define typed tool interfaces.Fix inputs, units, output schemas, required parameters, and failure messages so the agent cannot silently
mis-call a tool (Section 4.2).
3. Specify the verification gate.Enumerate the constraint-satisfaction checks (voltage/thermal limits, power balance, solver status) and
the faithfulness checks (every reported number traces to tool output) that the final response must pass (5).
4. Implement safe failure.When verification fails, abstain and report the failure mode and its cause rather than fabricating, extrapolating,
or silently repairing a value.
5. Choose the orchestration unit by task structure.A single-agent SDK for assistants, a multi-agent framework for genuinely distinct
roles, or a workflow graph when stages must be verified before execution (Section 4.5).
6. Manage memory deliberately.Keep a short-term scratchpad within a task and promote only verified results to long-term memory
(Section 4.4).
7. Evaluate on all four metric groups.Report task utility, solver-grounded correctness, faithfulness and safe failure, and cost and latency
against an LLM-only baseline on identical data (Section 5).
